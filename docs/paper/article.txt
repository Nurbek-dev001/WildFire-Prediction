An Explainable Multisource Machine Learning
Framework for Spatiotemporal Wildfire Risk
Prediction and Mapping in Kazakhstan
Samat Zharylkap and Nurbek Bolarbek
School of Data Science and Artificial Intelligence, Astana IT University,
Astana, Kazakhstan.
1 Introduction
A wildfire is an uncontrolled fire that burns vegetation, such as grass, shrubs, or trees.
Its development depends on fuel, moisture, weather, and an ignition source. Wildfire
occurrence prediction estimates whether a new event is likely within a specific area
and time. It differs from detection, which identifies a fire that has already started, and
spread prediction, which estimates how an existing fire may move [1].
Our motivation comes from the large wildfire that began in June 2023 in the Semei
Ormany reserve in Kazakhstan’s Abay Region. The disaster caused the deaths of 14
forestry workers and required emergency action [2]. It raised an important question
for our research: could reliable daily risk information help authorities prepare before
dangerous conditions develop into a major emergency? A prediction model cannot
guarantee that a fire will not start, and this study cannot establish that a model would
have prevented the 2023 disaster. However, trustworthy forecasts could support closer
monitoring, preparedness, and response planning. This possibility motivates our focus
on locally relevant predictions rather than only general hazard maps.
The environmental context also supports this idea. Some studies explain that
climate, vegetation, land use, and human activity jointly shape fire patterns.[3]. In
Central Asia also found frequent combined drought and heatwave events in north-
central Kazakhstan and linked compound conditions with vegetation losses [4]. These
studies does not predict fires, but it supports considering weather, vegetation, and
moisture together.

Related studies provide several useful starting points. [5] used Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) and Convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM) networks to predict next-
day wildfire danger in the Eastern Mediterranean using weather, vegetation, and
human-related data. [6] developed a multisource dataset for Morocco and evaluated
machine-learning models, including XGBoost and LightGBM, alongside feedforward
neural networks for localized wildfire prediction. In Kazakhstan, [7, 8] provide national
fire-risk and hazard-mapping frameworks. These studies show the value of regional
data and machine learning. However, they do not test the combined reliability of
daily probabilities, explanations, and transfer across Kazakhstan’s regions and future
seasons.
The purpose of this study is to fill these gaps and develop and evaluate an explain-
able multisource machine-learning framework for next-day vegetation-fire occurrence
prediction and mapping in Kazakhstan. Unlike image detection or broad hazard map-
ping alone, this work will study whether daily probabilities remain useful across time
and geography. Its target is the first satellite detection of a new event, which is a
practical indicator rather than an exact record of ignition.
The research will address the following questions:
1. Does combining weather, vegetation, terrain, land-cover, and human-related vari-
ables improve next-day prediction compared with seasonal fire frequency and a
weather-only model?
2. How well do the models perform on future years and geographically separate regions
of Kazakhstan?
3. Are the predicted probabilities well calibrated, and which factors explain the model
outputs across different regions and land-cover groups?
The proposed approach uses Kazakhstan data from 2015–2024, combining obser-
vations from NASA’s Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS)
and the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) with weather, vegetation,
terrain, land-cover, and dated human-related information[9]. The study will compare
Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and CatBoost against seasonal fire
frequency and a weather-only logistic models under the same data and validation
conditions. It will also examine whether vegetation, human-related information, and
recent weather history add value beyond simpler reference models. These comparisons
will connect model performance with the information used, rather than assuming that
a more complex method is always better. Evaluation combines average precision (AP),
F1, Brier skill, calibration checks, and uncertainty estimates.
The value of this study is practical as well as methodological. The Abay Region
disaster gives the research a clear national motivation, but the proposed framework
will consider Kazakhstan as a whole. Reliable daily information could help iden-
tify locations that need closer monitoring and support the planning of prevention
activities. Explanations could help users check whether predictions reflect reasonable
environmental patterns.
The proposed contribution is substantial because data quality, fair testing, proba-
bility reliability, and explanations are considered together. The value of the work will
depend on honest comparisons and reproducible evidence, including negative results

2 Literature Review
Wildfire occurrence reflects the interaction of combustible vegetation, moisture avail-
ability, weather, ignition sources, and landscape structure. Prediction consequently
requires more than identifying hot or dry conditions: it must account for where fuel
is available, how its condition changes, and where ignition is likely. Remote sensing
and machine learning (ML) offer complementary capabilities, providing repeated envi-
ronmental observations and modeling nonlinear relationships among heterogeneous
predictors [1, 10]. Nevertheless, the reliability of a predictive map depends on its tar-
get, observational labels, and validation design rather than on algorithmic complexity
alone.
Occurrence forecasting, susceptibility mapping, detection, and spread prediction
represent distinct tasks. Susceptibility describes spatial predisposition to fire; fore-
casting introduces a defined future time interval; detection identifies an existing fire;
and spread modeling estimates subsequent propagation. Satellite-based reviews and
spread-modeling surveys illustrate why their outputs and performance measures are
not directly interchangeable [11, 12]. Moreover, occurrence probability represents only
one component of risk. Exposure and vulnerability determine whether a hazardous
event threatens people, infrastructure, or ecosystems [13]. This distinction is essential
when interpreting probability maps as decision-support products.

2.1 Multisource Observations and Fire Drivers
Satellite observations provide both fire labels and environmental predictors. Thermal
anomalies indicate active burning, whereas vegetation indices, land cover, surface tem-
perature, and moisture products characterize fuel condition. Their integration with
precipitation, humidity, temperature, wind, and terrain allows models to represent
interactions between environmental dryness and spatial susceptibility [1, 14]. However,
a thermal detection is an observation of combustion, not necessarily an ignition record.
Persistent burning, agricultural fires, industrial heat, and missed observations compli-
cate label construction. Models trained on these labels must therefore distinguish the
intended vegetation-fire target from the satellite observation process [10].
Localized dataset construction addresses another source of uncertainty. Jadouli and
El Amrani integrate vegetation, soil moisture, meteorological, and population-related
observations for next-day prediction in Morocco, demonstrating that regional data
preparation is itself a substantial contribution [6]. Ahajjam et al. link spatiotempo-
ral clustering with ensemble prediction in Alaska, highlighting the relevance of event
organization when repeated detections describe the same fire [15]. These approaches
suggest that dataset quality requires coherent spatial and temporal units, not simply
a larger collection of hotspot records.
Weather and fuel indicators represent related but non-identical processes. Temper-
ature, humidity, precipitation, and drought affect drying; vegetation indices describe
vegetation condition; soil moisture represents antecedent water availability; and
wind influences potential propagation. Mambile et al. identify CNNs and LSTMs
as frequently used architectures and emphasize meteorological and satellite-derived
predictors, while Xu et al. distinguish time-series, segmentation, and spatiotemporal
formulations [1, 14]. Both reviews support heterogeneous data integration, but archi-
tecture classifications do not establish that one model is universally preferable across
these tasks.
Human-access variables complement environmental predictors because favorable
burning conditions do not necessarily produce ignition. Road proximity, settlements,
population density, and land use may describe human pressure, although they can
also correlate with reporting and observation intensity. Iban and Aksu examine con-
tributing factors through SHAP-based susceptibility mapping, while Bouzeraa et al.
integrate GIS and ML in a regional predictive framework [16, 17]. Their relevance lies
in connecting spatial predictions with identifiable drivers. The broader reviews identify
anthropogenic information and reproducibility as areas requiring further development
[14, 18].
2.2 Predictive Models and Spatial Outputs
Tree ensembles are attractive for tabular environmental data because they accom-
modate nonlinear interactions without requiring image-based inputs. Deep networks
instead offer representations suited to imagery and sequential observations. Ejaz and
Choudhury place both within a pipeline spanning acquisition, preprocessing, feature
engineering, evaluation, mapping, and deployment [10]. Galv´ an Lara et al. similarly
examine algorithmic configurations, data practices, and reproducibility, indicating that

fair comparison must control task definitions and experimental conditions [18]. Biswas
et al. directly compare deep and ML ensembles for California susceptibility prediction,
providing a relevant comparative setting rather than justification for assuming that
deeper architectures are automatically superior [19].
Gupta and Kim apply U-Net, DeepLabV3, and pyramid scene parsing networks to
geospatial wildfire-risk modeling using imagery and environmental layers [20]. Their
contribution is a spatial modeling proof of concept; its applicability depends on the
quality and meaning of the reference risk layers. Ouazri et al. combine CatBoost
and Transformer components with meteorological, fire-danger, and satellite observa-
tions and report 92.5% accuracy and ROC-AUC of 0.972 in Northern Morocco [21].
These results support hybrid prediction and explanation, but do not establish equiv-
alent operational performance under a different regional distribution or natural event
prevalence.
Choi and Chae address a different question: where an existing ignition is likely to
burn. Their U-Net spatial allocation and boosted area-regression components produce
a burn-probability field using terrain, fuel, and meteorology. Per-fire precision–recall
AUC reaches 0.429 against 0.321 for the strongest baseline, while aggregate calibra-
tion error is 0.025 [22]. The explicitly evaluated probability field is a strength, but
high-probability overconfidence and limited representation of suppression and spotting
restrict fine-scale interpretation. Its contribution informs probability-map evaluation
without substituting for pre-ignition occurrence forecasting.
Hybrid physical–ML systems seek greater behavioral structure. Benhamza et al.
connect neural ignition prediction with Cellular Automata propagation, joining pre-
dicted ignition locations to a spatial simulation [23]. Jadouli and El Amrani embed
entropy-inspired environmental representations within a neural network and examine
fire-triangle feature organization [24]. These studies connect prediction with domain
knowledge, although physical inspiration alone does not establish mechanistic valid-
ity or geographic transferability. Saleem et al. combine neural feature extraction and
XGBoost for multiclass disaster classification [25]. That architecture offers a related
methodological idea, but its multi-disaster target cannot be treated as direct evidence
of wildfire-occurrence forecasting skill.
2.3 Generalization, Calibration, and Explanation
Rare-event imbalance makes accuracy insufficient: a model may classify most non-fire
observations correctly while missing consequential fires. Camacho et al. investigate
resampling with ML for rare, high-impact wildfire prediction, demonstrating the
importance of sampling design [26]. However, large-fire prediction and general occur-
rence prediction concern different targets. Precision, recall, precision–recall AUC, and
evaluation at realistic prevalence are therefore needed alongside discrimination mea-
sures [10, 14]. Calibration adds a separate requirement: probability magnitude should
correspond to observed frequency, not merely rank locations correctly [22].
Spatial autocorrelation and temporal dependence also complicate validation.
Neighboring locations or repeated detections can make random splits insufficient
evidence of transfer to new regions or future seasons. Reviews identify geographic
bias, heterogeneous resolutions, distribution shifts, and inconsistent benchmarking as
5

--- page 6 ---
persistent obstacles [1, 10, 18]. Comparison should consequently distinguish within-
region interpolation from temporal forecasting and spatial transfer, while preventing
information from future observations or preprocessing stages from entering training.
Explainability makes these checks more interpretable, but does not replace them.
SHAP-based regional studies identify associations between predictors and model out-
puts, not necessarily causal ignition mechanisms [16, 21]. Apostolakis et al. connect
daily occurrence prediction with probability-based susceptibility levels and represen-
tative sampling for local explanations [27]. This strengthens the link between model
output and usable maps. Nevertheless, stable explanations across held-out regions and
seasons are more informative than feature rankings derived only from the training
distribution.
2.4 Kazakhstan Context and Research Implications
Kazakhstan already has relevant national-scale wildfire research. Babu et al. inte-
grate a Random Forest static hazard component with dynamic fire-weather, NDVI,
and fuel-moisture information in a spatiotemporal fire-risk index [7]. Chepashev et al.
develop a machine-learning and remote-sensing fire-hazard map [8]. These contribu-
tions establish regional precedents; therefore, novelty cannot be claimed simply from
applying ML in Kazakhstan. A more defensible direction is to examine the integration
of time-specific occurrence probabilities, anthropogenic information, regional transfer,
calibration, and explanations within a common evaluation framework.
Nurtas et al. review satellite-based DL for environmental-disaster detection with a
Kazakhstan focus, situating wildfire analysis within wider optical and radar applica-
tions [28]. Its broader hazard scope supports regional data and transfer considerations
but does not independently demonstrate wildfire forecasting performance. Likewise,
Wilk-Jakubowski et al. synthesize digital monitoring across fires, floods, earthquakes,
and air pollution, connecting prediction with sensors and monitoring technologies [29].
Operational usefulness requires timely observations and reliable workflows as well as
retrospective predictive skill.
Finally, probability maps should remain distinct from comprehensive impact assess-
ments. Dossi et al. identify infrastructure-sector gaps and the need to connect hazard,
exposure, vulnerability, and interdependencies [13]. Berchtold et al. introduce man-
agement tipping points to explain why extreme events can overwhelm conventional
measures and require layered strategies [30]. These perspectives support spatial deci-
sion assistance while cautioning against interpreting occurrence probability as a
complete measure of expected loss.
Taken together, existing research supports localized multisource modeling but
leaves important questions about label quality, human influences, transferability, prob-
ability reliability, and interpretation. For Kazakhstan, the central research need is not
an unnecessarily complex architecture, but an explainable spatiotemporal framework
whose probabilities remain credible across locations and seasons. Such a focus con-
nects environmental prediction with interpretable mapping without presupposing the
methodology or claiming outcomes before empirical evaluation.

3 Methods and Materials
3.1 Research Design
The proposed study will use a quantitative, retrospective modeling design with con-
trolled computational experiments. The objective is to estimate the probability of a
newly detected vegetation-fire event within a Kazakhstan grid cell during the following
day. This design is appropriate because satellite archives and environmental records
permit repeated evaluation across regions and seasons without conducting hazardous
ignition experiments. The framework will adapt multisource dataset construction and
comparative learning from regional wildfire studies, while strengthening temporal sep-
aration and probability evaluation [6, 7, 21]. All procedures below describe a planned
study; no model performance or completed experiment is assumed.
Prediction target and scope.
The initial analysis will cover 2015–2024 on a national equal-area grid with 5 km cells.
This resolution balances national computational requirements against the approxi-
mately 9 km native resolution of ERA5-Land; interpolation will not be interpreted
as additional meteorological detail [31]. A 1 km regional sensitivity experiment may
subsequently examine aggregation effects. Daily intervals will use UTC consistently
throughout the historical record. All months will be retained initially, with seasonal
performance reported separately rather than assuming that winter detections are
impossible. For eligible celliand target dayt, the model will estimate

reported separately. The output represents occurrence hazard, not expected damage
or a complete exposure–vulnerability assessment.
Event construction and sampling.
VIIRS detections will first be deduplicated and linked into events through spatial and
temporal proximity, following the event-oriented principle used by Ahajjam et al. [15].
Initial linkage settings will be 2 km and 48 hours, with 1–5 km and 24–72-hour sensi-
tivity tests. These values are proposed settings rather than thresholds established for
Kazakhstan. Only the earliest detection of each connected event will define its posi-
tive cell-day. At the daily prediction cutoff, cells with a detection during the preceding
48 hours will be temporarily excluded from the new-event risk set; this exclusion uses
past observations only. Event grouping may use later detections to identify repeated
observations for labeling, but those later observations will never enter predictors.
All eligible positives will be retained. To reduce storage and training costs, negative
cell-days will be sampled within month and broad land-cover strata using documented
inclusion probabilitiesq h. Approximately ten negatives per positive will be targeted,

with a minimum allocation to strata containing no observed fires. Inverse-inclusion
weights 1/qh will represent the original risk set. Sensitivity analyses will compare this
weighting with training-only class weighting. Validation and test data will remain
unbalanced; if complete evaluation is computationally infeasible, probability-sampled
evaluation will use corresponding weights. Absence of a detection will be described as
an observed negative, not independently verified absence of fire.
Models and experimental controls.
Four classifiers will be compared: L2-regularized logistic regression, Random Forest,
XGBoost, and CatBoost. Logistic regression provides an interpretable reference, while
tree ensembles accommodate nonlinear interactions and heterogeneous predictors, con-
sistent with regional comparison studies [16, 21]. Additional references will include
a smoothed land-cover–calendar-month fire-frequency predictor and a weather-only
logistic model. All methods will receive identical splits and feature information. Exper-
iments will remove anthropogenic variables, lagged weather features, and vegetation
information separately to quantify their incremental value. Resampling will be con-
sidered only inside training folds, informed by Camacho et al.; synthetic samples will
not be allowed in evaluation sets [26]. A neural–boosting architecture is not required
merely because it has performed well on a different multiclass disaster task [25].
Validation, tuning, and calibration.
Data from 2015–2021 will constitute model-development data. Hyperparameters will
be selected through rolling-origin validation, for example training through 2018 and
validating on 2019, then extending training for successive validation years through
2021. Fifty Optuna trials per ensemble will explore tree depth, minimum leaf size,
learning rate, regularization, and estimator count, selecting weighted average precision.
Early stopping will use development-fold validation only. After model selection, each
classifier will be fitted on all development years. The year 2022 will be reserved for
sigmoid probability calibration and decision-threshold selection; 2023–2024 will remain
untouched until final evaluation.
A separate five-fold spatial-block experiment within the development period
will assess geographic transfer. Initial 100 km blocks will be grouped into geo-
graphically separated folds, with a 25 km exclusion buffer around held-out blocks.
Block-size sensitivity will test 50 and 150 km partitions. Preprocessing and tuning
will be repeated within each fold. This design separates temporal forecasting from
spatial transfer and extends the geographic holdout principle examined in physics-
embedded wildfire modeling [24]. Probability calibration will use representative or
inverse-probability-weighted 2022 data rather than a balanced sample.
Explanation and mapping.
TreeSHAP will explain the selected tree ensemble using global mean absolute con-
tributions, dependence plots, and local examples from high-scoring and misclassified
cells [16]. Explanations will refer explicitly to the uncalibrated model score or log-
odds, not automatically to calibrated probabilities. Correlated predictor groups will
also be examined together. Contributions will be compared across land-cover groups

and held-out regions; they will be interpreted as predictive associations rather than
causal effects. Daily calibrated probabilities will be exported directly to GeoTIFF and
inspected in QGIS without interpolating point predictions. Following the probability-
based mapping motivation of Apostolakis et al., class boundaries will be selected on
calibration data and fixed before testing, with continuous probabilities and observed
class frequencies retained [27].
3.2 Data Sources
Fire observations and quality control.
NASA FIRMS archived, standard-processed Suomi-NPP VIIRS 375 m active-fire
observations will provide the primary labels. Using one primary sensor reduces
changing-sensor coverage effects. Nominal- and high-confidence detections will be
retained; low-confidence records will be excluded. VIIRS confidence categories will not
be treated as MODIS percentage values [9]. Coordinates, dates, confidence, and avail-
able source-type fields will be checked. Persistent industrial hotspots will be screened
against mapped non-vegetated locations, with the mask specified using development-
period evidence only. MODIS observations and burned-area products will provide
corroboration rather than indiscriminately adding detections to the target. Official
incident records, if obtainable with permission, will serve as supplementary verifica-
tion. They are not assumed to be available, and sample counts will be reported only
after extraction.
Environmental predictors.
ERA5-Land hourly fields will supply temperature, dewpoint, wind components, pre-
cipitation, and surface-layer soil moisture [31]. Daily temperature extrema, mean wind
speed, relative humidity derived from temperature and dewpoint, and precipitation
totals will be calculated before the target day. Accumulated precipitation will be con-
verted according to the product’s accumulation conventions. Seven- and fourteen-day
antecedent precipitation and temperature summaries will represent drying history.
Any optional fire-weather index will be computed exclusively from earlier observations
or introduced as a clearly documented archived product, not as target-day realized
weather.
MODIS MOD13A2 Version 6.1 will provide 1 km, 16-day NDVI and EVI with
quality masks [32]. A composite will enter predictors only after its full observation
interval has ended, using an additional conservative publication lag recorded in the
data manifest. Annual MCD12Q1 land cover will be used as fractions of forest, grass-
land, shrubland, cropland, and other classes; the preceding year’s map will be preferred
to avoid contemporaneous retrospective labeling. SRTM terrain will supply elevation,
slope, and aspect. Population density, settlement proximity, and road proximity will
be obtained from dated WorldPop products and historical OpenStreetMap snapshots
where available. Present-day road data will not be silently substituted for historical
conditions; unavailable historical layers will be omitted from the primary experiment
and clearly identified in sensitivity analyses.

Preparation, availability, and resources.
All layers will be clipped to an authoritative Kazakhstan boundary and aligned to a
documented equal-area projection. Continuous layers will be area-averaged or interpo-
lated appropriately; categorical layers will be summarized as area fractions. Slope will
be derived at native terrain resolution before aggregation. Vegetation gaps will use the
latest valid historical value for at most 32 days, after which missingness will be pre-
served. Numeric imputation, scaling for logistic regression, categorical encoding, and
feature screening will be fitted on training partitions only. Strongly correlated features
will be assessed with grouped ablations rather than automatically removing physically
informative fire drivers. The multisource preparation strategy follows regional dataset
and geospatial modeling approaches [6, 20].
The core inputs are publicly accessible under provider-specific terms; public access
does not imply unrestricted redistribution. No personal-level or self-collected data are
required. Python, pandas, xarray, GeoPandas, rasterio, scikit-learn, XGBoost, Cat-
Boost, Optuna, SHAP, and QGIS will support processing and analysis. Google Earth
Engine may assist raster extraction, subject to account access and quotas. A mul-
ticore workstation with approximately 32 GB RAM and SSD storage is a proposed
starting configuration, not a claim about available hardware. Partitioned Parquet files
and chunked raster processing will control memory use; larger runs may use approved
cloud resources. Actual hardware, package versions, random seeds, source identifiers,
exclusions, and split manifests will accompany the final reproducibility record.

Positive BSS indicates improvement over the reference. Reliability diagrams and ten-

Map usefulness will be assessed by the proportion of detected event starts captured
